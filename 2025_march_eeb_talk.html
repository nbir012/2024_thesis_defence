Factors which influence likelihood of eating insects (logistic regression)

Unconscious Bias

  • Asked people to list the first words or phrases that came to mind when they saw a particular menu ingredient (e.g. beef, tofu, silkworm pupae)
  • Despite participants overall willingness to eat insects, insect ingredients had significantly lower sentiment scores in the words used to describe them.

Unconscious Bias

Chapter 4: The metabolomic fingerprint of an edible insect species, P. reticularis, along a latitudinal gradient

Can we use the metabolomic profile of an insect to determine which geographic location it came from?

  • collecting grubs along a latitudinal gradient
  • Used GC-MS along with two derivatisation techniques to identify metabolites
  • Assessed which derivatisation technique had was most effective at predicting correct location of a species

Findings (PERMANOVA)

Methyl chloroformate (MCF):

  • Location, larval weight, log and log temperature were all significant.
    • Location only explained 12.3% of the variation in the model

Trimethylsilyl (TMS):

  • Only location was significant.
    • Location explained 31.1% of the variation in the model

Ramdom Forest Classification Model

  • TMS had the highest accuracy, predicting the correct location of a sample 72.2% of the time
  • MCF had a lower accuracy of 68.1%
    • The field site of Kaingaroa had the highest error rate which impacted overall accuracy

Findings

  • Wing loading (PW and WAR) was significant for all three measures of flight performance
  • incr. wing loading is associated with fast but inefficient flight

Sexual dimorphism

  • Male antennae were significantly larger than female antennae
  • Females were significantly larger in all other traits.

Insect Conservation in NZ

  • There has long been concern around anthropogenic change and insect numbers in NZ
  • Insect conservation received greater attention in the 1980’s

  • there has been an awareness of a reduction in insect numbers driven by anthropogenic changes since at least the 1880 with Hudson’s concern of forest burning
  • Commander James J. Walker (1921) in his presidential address to the Entomological Society of London voiced concern for NZ invertebrates
  • ‘the disastrous effect on all forms of life of this destruction and replacement … (necessitate) immediate and thorough research in what still remains of the endemic animal life of New Zealand, before many of its most interesting forms are lost forever.’
  • with an increased understanding of the need to conserve insects in the late 20th century

Insect Apocalypse

  • There is an “Insect Apocalypse” narrative present in popular media
  • Based on a small number of geographically and taxonomically limited long-term studies
  • Saunders et al. (2020) advise caution and suggest steps required to investigate this

  • Saunders et al. propose Taxonomy, natural history observations, and population dynamics of insect species amongst other areas

Aotearoa

  • Only 1/4 of insects described
  • Coleoptera estimated to have 10,000 species
  • Only 1/2 of which are described
  • Lots of unusual species thanks to our island biogeography
  • Frequent mentions in the literature of a paucity of taxonomists

Fellowship proposal looks at Anagotus

  • 18 described species
  • ~ 11 undescribed species
  • Found in Alpine, sub-alpine, lowland and off shore islands
  • Three species are protected under the Wildlife Act (1953)
  • One of those protected species is Anagotus fairburni

Conservation hyper success/failure?

  • Anagotus fairburni was released on Mana Island
  • 2004 and 2006 releases of adults onto flax bushes on the island
  • By 2018 the weevils had destroyed large areas of flax
  • Put at risk important food and habitat for endangered birds and lizards
  • This highlights the importance of understanding the ecology of these species… was a parisitoid missed on translocation
  • Perfect opportunity to use modelling to understand ecological drivers of these systems

Fellowship proposal looks at Anagotus

  • Aim 1: Comprehensive revision and cataloguing of the genus Anagotus

  • Aim 2: Comparative analysis of Anagotus species

  • Aim 3: Predicted distribution of Anagotus in New Zealand

  • Aim 4: Factors leading to the range reduction of Anagotus in New Zealand

Aim 1: Revision and cataloguing of the genus

  • Examine morphological traits to delineate species
  • Construct a taxonomic key and a character matrix for cladistic analysis
  • Work in partnership with iwi or hapū for species description
  • Catalogue all Anagotus specimens and associated label data from NZAC and other collections

“An inordinate fondness for beetles (and their penises)”

Aim 2: Comparative analysis of Anagotus species

  • Field work at alpine, subalpine, lowland, and offshore island locations
  • Measure, mark, and release individuals to determine lifespan, dispersal capability, and survival rate
  • Employ behavioural sampling techniques to assess differences between species
  • This will help to identify morph/behavioural traits that may be associated adaptability to ecosystems in flux
  • Also opportunities to generate new hypotheses

Aim 3: Predicted distribution of Anagotus in New Zealand

  • Use environmental data and presence-only species data
  • Simulate SDMs using ensemble models
  • Interrogate models using xAI techniques
  • This will help to identify traits that may be associated adaptability to ecosystems in flux
  • Also opportunities to generate new hypotheses

Aim 3: Predicted distribution of Anagotus in New Zealand

  • databased over 1000 pinned specimens in the NZAC
  • Allows me to map out locations, last date collected etc.

Aim 4: Factors leading to the range reduction of Anagotus in New Zealand

  • Use SEPMs to simulate movement, mortality, and reproduction
  • Build SEPMs using the R package ‘poems’
  • Evaluate models using a pattern-oriented modelling framework
  • Damien A. Fordham et al. Sean Haythorne, Stuart C. Brown, Jessie C. Buettel, Barry W. Brook
  • Used hunting and bounty dynamics to model the historic decline of the thylacine in Tasmania, Australia
  • Work with George Perry to explore what has led to range shifts in this group
  • Once found across the motu they are now pushed to alpine/off shore island habitats.
